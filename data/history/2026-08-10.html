Detailed writeup

What actually changed

For decades, F-1 students were admitted for "Duration of Status" (D/S) — their legal stay tracked their I-20 program dates, and a Designated School Official (DSO) could extend that end date directly in SEVIS for a compelling academic or medical reason, with no separate federal filing required. The new rule ends that. Beginning September 15, 2026, F-1 students receive a fixed "Admit Until Date" on their I-94, set to the length of their program as listed on the I-20 — capped at four years for undergraduate and doctoral programs, with shorter caps typical for master's-level programs — plus a 30-day grace period to arrive and another 30 days after program completion.

Why this matters for someone who might need an extra semester

Previously, running long on a degree was mostly an institutional matter: the DSO updated the SEVIS record and issued a new I-20, and that was sufficient to stay in status. Under the new regime there are two separate actions:

  1. DSO program extension (unchanged in spirit): the DSO can still extend the Program End Date in SEVIS for a compelling academic or medical reason, or DSO error — SEVIS allows this without SEVP adjudication, for up to one additional year.
  2. Extension of Stay with USCIS (new requirement): the student must separately file Form I-539 to extend the actual admit-until date on their I-94 before it expires. Missing this deadline — even if the SEVIS/I-20 record is fine — risks accruing unlawful presence, which has serious consequences for future visas and re-entry.
Practical risk: USCIS adjudication of Form I-539 is not instant, and the rule requires that USCIS receive the filing before the current admission period expires. A student who realizes late in a semester that they'll need extra time has a narrower runway than under the old D/S system — the DSO extension and the USCIS filing both need to happen with time to spare.

New restrictions to be aware of

The rule also tightens what students can change while extending: F-1 graduate students are now barred from changing programs, majors, or education levels altogether (absent a narrow SEVP exception for extenuating circumstances). First-year undergraduates face a similar bar on changing programs/majors/levels within their first year unless SEVP grants an exception. This is relevant if an extra semester is being used to also switch a concentration, add a second major, or move between degree levels (e.g., BS to accelerated MS).

Transition relief for students already here

Students who were in the U.S. under D/S before September 15, 2026 are not immediately forced to file an EOS the moment the rule takes effect. But if OPT or STEM OPT is part of the plan, the relief is time-boxed: Form I-765 (with I-539 bundled in for the relevant period, where applicable) must be filed by March 18, 2027 to use the simplified transition path instead of a stand-alone up-front EOS application.

Where things stand politically and legally

NAFSA and a broad coalition of higher-education associations (American Council on Education, APLU, NACAC, and others) opposed the proposed version of this rule in comments last year. NAFSA's CEO has said the association will "explore every available avenue" to challenge the final rule, but as of this check no lawsuit had been filed. Because it's classified as a "major rule," Congress has an opportunity to review and potentially disapprove it under the Congressional Review Act — no such resolution has passed as of this check. Bottom line: the rule is real, published, and on track for September 15, 2026 unless a court or Congress intervenes before then.
