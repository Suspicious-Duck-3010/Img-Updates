Detailed writeup

1. UPDATED — DHS Final Rule ending Duration of Status

Confirmed (official) · first tracked 2026-08-10 · last updated 2026-08-14 · re-checked 2026-08-14

For decades, F-1 students were admitted for "Duration of Status" (D/S) — their legal stay tracked their I-20 program dates, and a Designated School Official (DSO) could extend that end date directly in SEVIS for a compelling academic or medical reason, with no separate federal filing required. The new rule ends that. Beginning September 15, 2026, F-1 students receive a fixed "Admit Until Date" on their I-94, set to the length of their program as listed on the I-20 — capped at four years for undergraduate and doctoral programs (24 months for language-training programs) — plus a 30-day grace period to arrive and another 30 days after program completion.

Why this matters for someone who might need an extra semester: there are now two separate actions — (1) the DSO can still extend the Program End Date in SEVIS for a compelling academic or medical reason, for up to one additional year; (2) the student must separately file Form I-539 with USCIS to extend the actual admit-until date before it expires. A timely-filed I-539 buys up to 240 days of authorized stay while pending, but missing the filing deadline risks accruing unlawful presence. New today: the I-539 EOS filing fee is reported at $420 if filed online / $470 by paper — a direct out-of-pocket cost that didn't exist under the old DSO-only SEVIS extension.

Practical risk: USCIS adjudication of Form I-539 is not instant, and the filing must be received before the current admission period expires. Combined with the new RFE/NOID policy below, a late or incomplete filing is now higher-stakes than under the old D/S system.

New restrictions: F-1 graduate students are barred from changing programs, majors, or education levels once enrolled (narrow SEVP exceptions only); first-year undergraduates face a similar bar. Relevant if an extra semester is being used to also switch a concentration or degree level.

Transition relief, with a hard outside limit: students in the U.S. under D/S before Sep 15, 2026 aren't immediately forced to file an EOS, and if they never leave and re-enter the U.S., they may remain until their I-20/OPT/STEM-OPT end date — but no later than Nov 14, 2030 (four years plus the 60-day grace period, measured from the Sep 15, 2026 effective date), a hard cap newly confirmed via secondary reporting this run. If OPT/STEM OPT is part of the plan, Form I-765 (with I-539 bundled where applicable) must be filed by Mar 18, 2027 to use the simplified transition path. Leaving and re-entering the U.S. on or after Sep 15, 2026 ends the transition protections immediately — CBP will instead issue a date-specific Admit Until Date with only the new 30-day grace period. Several outlets (e.g. Forbes/Stuart Anderson, Aug 3, 2026) additionally report a "6-month transition reprieve" for OPT/STEM OPT filers; this detail still could not be verified against a directly-rendered official document today, so treat it as reported, not confirmed.

Where things stand: NAFSA and higher-ed associations remain publicly opposed to the rule (see item 5 below for their latest enrollment-impact projection), but as of this run (checked again 2026-08-14) no lawsuit has been filed and no CRA disapproval resolution has passed — reporting still frames both as merely "possible" or "expected," not filed. A direct congress.gov search for a CRA joint resolution of disapproval could not be rendered today either, so this remains based on secondary reporting. SEVP has scheduled a stakeholder webinar for Aug 31, 2026 (2–3pm ET, no registration required, capped at 1,000 participants, recording to be posted afterward). A parallel Department of State / BridgeUSA guidance directive for J-1 sponsors (reported issued Aug 10, 2026) is a further sign that federal agencies are actively standing up implementation ahead of the effective date, though it's J-1-specific and unconfirmed against state.gov.

Consistent advice: talk to your DSO now if there's any chance of needing more time, so the SEVIS extension and USCIS filing aren't both rushed against a hard deadline.

3. USCIS: deny-without-RFE/NOID policy (PA-2026-05)

Reported, unconfirmed · first tracked 2026-08-10 · effective (reported) 2026-08-05 · re-checked 2026-08-14

Multiple immigration-law sources report that USCIS updated Policy Manual guidance, effective August 5, 2026, giving adjudicators discretion to deny a benefit request that lacks required initial evidence or otherwise fails to establish eligibility — without first issuing a Request for Evidence (RFE) or Notice of Intent to Deny (NOID). Officers may also set RFE response deadlines shorter than the prior 12-week maximum, and the 14-day international-mail grace period for RFE responses has reportedly been eliminated. USCIS reportedly frames this as a response to incomplete or placeholder filings driving adjudication delays.

Why it matters here: under the new fixed-admission-period regime, F-1 students needing an extra semester must file Form I-539 (Extension of Stay) — and OPT/STEM OPT transition filers use Form I-765. A materially incomplete package can now be denied outright rather than getting a chance to supplement the record, raising the cost of filing errors right when more students will be filing EOS applications for the first time.
