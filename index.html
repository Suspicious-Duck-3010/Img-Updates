1. NEW: DHS proposes a $103,265 fee on cap-subject H-1B petitions -- comment period closes Sept 24, 2026 -- directly relevant to F-1 students' OPT-to-H-1B transition

Confirmed · first tracked 2026-09-23 · last updated 2026-09-23 · re-checked 2026-09-23

On Aug 25, 2026, DHS published a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (doc. 2026-17324) proposing a new $103,265 fee, payable at time of filing, for every H-1B cap-subject petition -- including petitions filed under the U.S. advanced-degree exemption -- in addition to all other existing fees. The fee would apply to petitions filed under both the regular 65,000 cap and the 20,000 advanced-degree exemption, but not to cap-exempt petitions, extensions, amendments, or transfers for beneficiaries already counted against the cap. Public comments are due by Sept 24, 2026 (tomorrow, relative to this run); if finalized, secondary reporting indicates the fee could apply to H-1B cap-selected petitions as early as April 1, 2027, unless litigation blocks it (immigration attorneys widely expect a new legal challenge, likely arguing the fee amount is arbitrary and capricious rather than a defensible cost-recovery figure). WHY THIS MATTERS for a VT student weighing an extra semester: this does not directly change F-1 status rules, but it strikes at the same population this tracker follows -- F-1 students who complete OPT/STEM OPT and hope to transition into H-1B status. A $103,265 per-petition cost (likely borne largely by employers, who may pass on hiring risk to candidates) could sharply reduce how many employers are willing to sponsor new H-1B cap petitions for OPT/STEM-OPT graduates, potentially leaving some students with fewer post-graduation options and adding pressure either to find alternative status paths or to extend time in F-1 status (e.g., via an additional degree or program) while employer sponsorship dynamics shake out. This compounds with the already-tracked NPRM to eliminate the discretionary 60-day grace period for H-1B/L-1/O-1/E/TN workers (item 'h1b-60day-grace-period-nprm-2026-09') -- together, the two proposals would make the OPT-to-H-1B pathway both more expensive to enter and less forgiving to exit if it doesn't work out. This is a proposed rule only; it is not yet in effect and has not (yet) been challenged in litigation.

Also watching whether Virginia Tech's Cranwell International Center or the VT Graduate School issue any guidance specific to OPT-to-H-1B transition planning in light of this proposal.

3. DHS Final Rule: Fixed Time Period of Admission & Extension of Stay (ends Duration of Status) for F, J, I nonimmigrants

Confirmed · first tracked 2026-08-10 · last updated 2026-09-15 · re-checked 2026-09-23

STATUS UPDATE (2026-09-15): although this final rule was published in the Federal Register on July 17, 2026 and was scheduled to take effect Sept 15, 2026, a federal court granted a NATIONWIDE PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION on Sept 14, 2026 blocking DHS/ICE from implementing it while litigation proceeds -- see item 'lawsuit-vs-ds-rule-2026-08' for full detail. As of this run, the rule's core change (ending duration of status in favor of a fixed admission period capped at 4 years) is NOT in effect; F, J, and I nonimmigrants continue to be admitted for duration of status under the pre-existing framework. This is a preliminary, not final, posture -- the rule remains validly published and could be reinstated on appeal or after further proceedings, potentially with little notice. Original rule background retained below.

DHS published a final rule (doc. 2026-14439) on Jul 17, 2026, ending open-ended Duration of Status for F-1 students, effective Sep 15, 2026. Students get a fixed 'Admit Until Date' capped at 4 years (24 months for language programs); anyone needing more time must file Form I-539 (Extension of Stay) with USCIS in addition to the existing DSO SEVIS extension -- the I-539 EOS filing fee is reported as $420 filed online / $470 by paper. A timely-filed EOS gives up to 240 days of authorized stay while pending. The rule also shortens the F-1 post-completion grace period from 60 to 30 days for anyone who travels/re-enters or gets an EOS approval after the effective date. Students already in D/S status who remain continuously in the U.S. without leaving/re-entering keep transition protections, but only up to a hard outside cap of Nov 14, 2030 (4 years + 60-day grace period measured from the Sep 15, 2026 effective date). DHS has discretion to postpone, until as late as Sep 14, 2028, implementation of the specific provisions restricting mid-program school transfers, changes in educational objective (major/level), and progression to same- or lower-level programs, if DHS determines full implementation isn't feasible by the Sep 15, 2026 effective date -- this discretionary postponement option applies only to those specific transfer/education-level provisions, not to the core fixed-admission-period change itself. As a major rule, it is also subject to Congressional Review Act review: Congress has a 60-day window after the rule is reported to introduce a joint resolution of disapproval, which -- if enacted -- would void the rule and bar a substantially similar rule absent new legislation; as of this run no such resolution has been reported as introduced. USCIS will publish revised editions of Form I-539 and Form I-765 effective Sep 15, 2026, aligned to the new fixed-admission-period process -- the current 08/28/24 edition of Form I-539 will still be accepted if postmarked or e-filed before Sep 15, 2026, but rejected on/after that date (no grace period), after which only the new 09/15/26 edition is accepted. On Aug 18, 2026, the anticipated legal challenge became a real lawsuit: NAFSA, the Presidents' Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration, and other higher-ed/labor groups filed a complaint and motion for preliminary injunction against the rule in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts (case assigned to District Judge F. Dennis Saylor, IV), seeking to stay and vacate it (or at minimum block its enforcement against plaintiffs and their members) before the Sep 15, 2026 effective date -- see item 'lawsuit-vs-ds-rule-2026-08' for full details. SEVP is hosting a live stakeholder webinar on Mon, Aug 31, 2026, 2-3pm ET to review the rule's major changes and answer FAQs. Virginia Tech's Office of the Provost and Cranwell International Center have now published VT-specific implementation guidance -- see the new item 'vt-provost-cic-guidance-2026-08' -- including a hard Aug 31, 2026 deadline for graduate students needing a program/major change or school transfer processed before the transition. NEW (2026-09-12): a GAO legal decision (B-338604, dated Aug 4, 2026) found that the rule's Sep 15, 2026 effective date does not appear to satisfy the Congressional Review Act's 60-day delay requirement for major rules, since the Senate received the rule Jul 20, 2026 (60 days would run to on/about Sep 18, 2026) -- an official, .gov-sourced procedural detail, not a court ruling, and DHS has not been reported changing its effective date as a result. The rule's effective date is now TOMORROW (Sep 15, 2026), with no DHS/SEVP announcement of any delay or postponement found. The previously-tracked discrepancy over SEVIS Release 6.89.1's deployment-window end time is now resolved in favor of Sep 14, 8:00 PM ET through Sep 15, 4:00 AM EDT, per Study in the States' own planning-guide content. Update 2026-09-18: USCIS's own I-539/I-765 pages (uscis.gov/i-539, uscis.gov/i-765) now carry a direct DHS statement confirming the rule's effective date is postponed and USCIS is proceeding under the pre-existing (D/S) framework pending further court order -- see item 'lawsuit-vs-ds-rule-2026-08' for the full quoted text. Relatedly, USCIS has reversed the Sept 15 form-edition rollout: filers are now instructed to continue using the 08/28/24 edition of Form I-539 and the 08/21/25 edition of Form I-765, since the 09/15/26 editions are not being accepted while the injunction stands -- see item 'ds-rule-operational-rollout-2026-09' for detail. No DOJ notice of appeal or stay motion found today; no new GAO/CRA development found today.

Mass.) granted nationwide preliminary relief in Presidents' Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration et al. v. DHS, 1:26-cv-13799 (D. The order blocks DHS and ICE from implementing the rule nationwide (not just for plaintiffs' own members) while the case proceeds, and the court reportedly wrote that 'the promulgation of the rule clearly failed to comply with the [Administrative Procedure Act],' calling the administration's stated national-security rationale weak and questioning whether the rule's real purpose was something else. As a result, F, J, and I nonimmigrants -- including F-1 students -- continue to be admitted for duration of status (D/S) for now, and the planned shift to a fixed admission period (capped at 4 years, tied to the I-20/DS-2019 program end date, requiring a separate Form I-539 extension-of-stay adjudication to go beyond it) has NOT taken effect. This is directly the scenario this tracker exists to watch: the enjoined rule is exactly the mechanism (a hard, USCIS-adjudicated admission-period cap) that would have squeezed a VT student needing an extra semester or two to finish. For now, the older, more forgiving D/S framework (extend by simply getting a new Program End Date on the I-20 from the DSO, no separate USCIS filing or adjudication needed just to keep pursuing 'normal progress') remains in place. IMPORTANT CAVEATS: (1) this is a PRELIMINARY injunction, not a final ruling -- the underlying legality of the rule has not been decided; (2) the government is widely expected to seek an emergency stay of the injunction and/or appeal to the First Circuit, and immigration counsel are advising students and employers to be prepared for the rule to be reinstated 'with little or no notice' if a higher court disagrees; (3) separately, and confusingly, USCIS and SEVP proceeded on Sept 14-15 with technical rollout tied to the now-enjoined rule anyway -- see new item 'ds-rule-operational-rollout-2026-09' -- so DSOs/students should watch for conflicting signals about which forms/processes currently apply. This tracker will keep monitoring for (a) any DHS emergency-stay motion or appeal, (b) any Federal Register notice postponing/updating the rule's status, and (c) Cranwell/VT guidance responding specifically to the injunction.

6. NEW: DHS proposes eliminating the discretionary 60-day grace period for H-1B, L-1, O-1, E, and TN workers -- shrinks the safety margin for F-1 students transitioning from OPT/STEM OPT into H-1B status

Confirmed · first tracked 2026-09-16 · last updated 2026-09-16 · re-checked 2026-09-23

On Sept 11, 2026, DHS published a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (doc. 2026-18631, 91 FR 57807; DHS Docket No. USCIS-2026-0364) proposing to remove 8 CFR 214.1(l)(2), which currently gives certain nonimmigrant workers -- H-1B, H-1B1, L-1, O-1, E-1, E-2, E-3, and TN, plus their dependents -- up to a 60-day discretionary grace period after their employment ends and before their authorized stay expires. If finalized as written, a worker in one of these classifications (and their spouse/children) would be considered to have failed to maintain status the day after employment ends, and would be expected to depart the U.S. immediately unless separately authorized to remain (e.g., a timely new petition or change of status). This does not directly change the F-1 grace period (that is a separate, already-tracked provision of the enjoined D/S final rule -- see items above), but it is highly relevant to the same population this tracker follows: F-1 students who complete OPT or STEM OPT and transition into H-1B status. Today, an F-1 student moving to H-1B after OPT typically still benefits from the general 60-day grace period once employment ends; if this NPRM is finalized, that buffer disappears for H-1B (and the other listed classifications), meaning a layoff or employer-side delay during the OPT-to-H-1B transition could leave far less room to find new work, change status, or depart, compounding pressure on anyone already extending their program via an extra semester. Public comments are due by Nov 10, 2026; this is a proposed rule only -- it is not yet in effect and has not been challenged in litigation.
